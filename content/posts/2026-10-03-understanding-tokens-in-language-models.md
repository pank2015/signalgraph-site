## What it is
A token is the smallest piece of text that a language model operates on. Raw text is first split into tokens by a tokenizer, and the model receives a sequence of token IDs. Think of a tokenizer as a translator that converts a sentence into a list of dictionary entries; each entry is a token. This step is necessary because neural networks work with fixed‑size numeric vectors, not raw characters.

## Why it matters
The way text is tokenized influences several system properties. A tokenizer that creates many short tokens increases the length of the input sequence, which raises compute and memory usage and can hit context‑window limits faster. Conversely, a tokenizer that creates longer tokens may reduce sequence length but can increase the vocabulary size, making the model larger and potentially raising out‑of‑vocabulary rates for rare words or languages. Token count also directly affects cost in many commercial APIs, where pricing is per input or output token. Efficient tokenization therefore impacts latency, throughput, and expense.

## How it works
Tokenization typically has two stages: pre‑tokenization and model‑specific tokenization. Pre‑tokenization splits text into words or punctuation using rule‑based boundaries (e.g., whitespace). The second stage applies a learned algorithm to convert those pieces into subword units. Two widely used algorithms are Byte‑Pair Encoding (BPE) and UnigramLM. BPE starts with individual bytes and iteratively merges the most frequent pair, building a vocabulary of subword strings. UnigramLM begins with a large candidate vocabulary and prunes items to maximize the likelihood of the training data. These methods differ along two orthogonal axes: their optimisation objective (compression vs. log‑likelihood) and their search procedure (bottom‑up merging vs. top‑down pruning) [S2]. Empirical work shows that the search procedure is the dominant factor for compression efficiency: bottom‑up tokenisers consistently achieve lower bits‑per‑byte in most settings, while neither axis predicts performance on the BLiMP linguistic benchmark [S2].

As a concrete example, take the word "unhappiness". After pre‑tokenization it remains a single word. A BPE tokenizer trained on English might have learned the subwords "un", "happ", and "iness". The word would then be represented as the token sequence ["un", "happ", "iness"], each mapped to an ID that the model consumes.

## Key techniques or variants
Besides BPE and Unigram, other strategies include WordPiece (similar to BPE but uses a likelihood‑based merge criterion), byte‑level BPE (operates directly on UTF‑8 bytes to avoid any unknown characters), and pure character‑level tokenization (each character is a token). Tokenizers are also evaluated with metrics beyond simple compression rate. The TokEval suite captures linguistically meaningful properties such as UTF‑8 character boundary integrity and digit place‑value boundary alignment for mathematics, and finds that information‑theoretic metrics correlate with language‑modeling ability (Spearman rho up to 0.80) while structure‑sensitive metrics correlate with task accuracy on tasks like math reasoning [S3].

## Applications
Tokens are central to any system that relies on large language models. In API‑based services, the number of tokens determines billing; for instance, GPT‑6.1 Sol is offered at one‑fifth of Astra’s standard API input and output token price [S8]. Large‑scale AI initiatives also quantify token consumption in credits, such as Google’s $40M commitment of AI tokens to the Genesis Mission [S9].

In enterprise settings, token‑hungry agents motivate architectural patterns that reduce token overhead. Teams use data mesh, low‑latency databases, semantic ontologies, and dynamic tool selection to shrink context windows and limit redundant token processing [S4]. Context‑engineering practices—like lazy‑loaded skills, versioned context artifacts, externalized memory banks, and LLM‑as‑a‑judge evaluation—aim to keep prompts within the model’s token limit while preserving necessary information [S7][S10].

For agentic workflows that repeatedly append small results to a growing prompt, stateful tokenization can avoid re‑tokenizing the entire history. Across two agent ecosystems, the median call appended about 1.4K characters, and only 1.0‑3.6% of calls started a session with multi‑million‑character contexts [S14]. With a 94.1% prompt‑cache hit rate, tokenization accounted for up to 64% of time‑to‑first‑token, showing the potential savings of incremental approaches [S14]. Across 17 tokenizer families, extensive validation confirmed zero divergence between stateful and full tokenization over billions of split checks and terabytes of text [S14].

Tokens also appear in system prompts that steer model behavior. An audit of 3,249 instructions from 88 commercial AI products found that protective instructions are present in 98.9% of products but only 24% cover all eight dimensions of the AISPA taxonomy, and some organizations average over 60 protective instructions per product while others average fewer than five [S6].

In specialized domains such as Korean public‑API tool chaining, researchers built a benchmark of 145 real‑world tasks (KOPA‑Bench) and showed that a 9B model fine‑tuned on synthesized trajectories nearly matches an untuned 27B model, illustrating how token‑efficient representations can support complex multi‑step reasoning [S11].

## Trade-offs and limitations
Choosing a tokenization scheme involves trade‑offs. A large vocabulary reduces the number of tokens per document but increases model size and memory footprint, and may still fail to encode very rare strings, leading to multiple fallback tokens or characters. Subword methods can split words in ways that hurt morphological clarity, especially for languages with rich inflection. Tokenizers that operate at the byte level guarantee coverage of any Unicode text but often produce longer sequences, raising computational cost.

Stateful tokenization saves repeat work but adds complexity: the service must maintain a cache, detect stable boundaries, and occasionally fall back to full tokenization when the assumption breaks. Token limits also constrain how much context can be supplied; when exceeded, engineers must rely on summarization, retrieval, or compression, each of which can lose information.

Cost modeling is sensitive to token price fluctuations and to the specific tokenization used by a provider, making cross‑system comparisons difficult. Finally, tokenization decisions are often made early in model training and are hard to change without retraining, locking in their effects for the model’s lifetime.
